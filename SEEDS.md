On the instrument itself

Marginalia is not an answer machine. The risk of any AI-adjacent tool is that it becomes a retrieval engine — you ask, it answers, you move on. The design intent is Socratic: a colleague at the same PD session, an off-the-record supervisor, a partner who asks the next question rather than closing the current one. This needs to be visible in every prompt, every synthesis, every interface decision. When the tool confirms your thesis, something has gone wrong.

Disembodied cognition studying embodied learning. The instrument was built using AI — a form of cognition without a body, without situatedness, without the phenomenological weight that the dissertation argues is essential to learning. The irony is not a problem to resolve. It is data. A researcher who built a largely-local AI research instrument to study why bodies matter in classrooms has something to say about the relationship between the tool and the inquiry. This belongs in the positionality section. It will also make a good reflective post.

The tool reflects the epistemological commitments of the research. Largely local is not just a technical choice — it is a methodological one. Community conversations as data raises participant privacy questions. Building the ethics board answer into the schema from the start (IRL capture provenance fields, capture_context, authors as idea source not just writer) makes methodological rigour visible to Hamilton before the proposal is submitted. The instrument is an argument.

Google Pinpoint comparison. Pinpoint (journaliststudio.google.com/pinpoint) does similar things — bulk document ingestion, Gemini Q&A against your own collection, collaborative research. The differences are telling. Pinpoint is a wall: you ask, it retrieves evidence. Marginalia is a winger: it holds your process, not just your documents. Pinpoint's privacy policy allows Google human reviewers to read your prompts and uses your interactions to improve their models. For community-sourced data with ethics board implications, that is a disqualifying condition. Largely local is the answer to a question Pinpoint doesn't ask.

On session design

Work is not a rabbit. But it can be a bear. An old colleague's framing: work will not run away. The counter: sometimes it needs to eat its fill before moving on. The instrument should hold both researchers — the one who needs permission to stop, and the one who needs the work to feel finished before they can. The nap mode, the step-away button, the boulder — these are not productivity tools. They are an acknowledgment that research has rhythms that schedulers do not understand.

The boulder never crests. The session timer animation — a small boulder rolling up a hill, driven by elapsed time, rolling back on step-away — should never quite reach the top. This is intentional. The hill is always there. The work is always incomplete in some meaningful sense. The instrument does not pretend otherwise. The boulder rolling back is not failure, it is rest.

The gradient bar and the boulder are independent. The status bar fills left to right with a warm dark gradient — #2a2018, earthy not urgent — as the session lengthens. The boulder rolls separately. The bar is the session's mass. The boulder is the session's motion. When you step away the boulder rolls back, the bar holds. The bar is a record. The boulder resets. Time spent does not disappear. Effort can begin again.

Angry colours are the wrong register. A research instrument that nags is a bad supervisor. The visual language should stay warm and Georgian throughout — the palette of the instrument, not the palette of a warning system. Text gets larger, not redder. The boulder gets higher, not faster. The instrument becomes more present as time passes, not more urgent.

The 2h session note prompt is Socratic by design. "Where are you? What's shifting?" is not a progress check. It is an invitation to surface what the session has actually done to your thinking, not what you planned it to do. The off-record supervisor asks this. The canonical record holds the answer. Over time the session notes become a longitudinal record of how the thinking moved — more useful than any single synthesis.

On the 60/40 principle

60/40 paper-to-process, or better. If the instrument captures more than it generates, the researcher's voice dominates the record. A research instrument that produces more AI text than researcher text has the epistemological balance backwards. The ingest-to-note flow (OCR → Save as Note), the deep read tab, the session prompts as thinking provocations rather than answer requests — these are all structural commitments to keeping the researcher's voice primary. The hint text on the capture screen says it plainly: Note = your thinking · Reference = someone else's work.

Notes and deep reading don't intend to go anywhere. The Notes tab holds accumulation without obligation. Some of it feeds writing eventually. Some of it just becomes part of how you think. The instrument needs to hold that kind of slow, purposeless, accumulative engagement — not just structured inquiry. Deep reading is the reps that build the muscle. The canonical type should reflect that: no destination field, no status workflow, just a body and the questions it raised.

On distribution

The curious researcher is the target. Not the developer. Not the non-technical user. The researcher who is motivated but protective of their machine internals — geek enough to be dangerous, not fully unix-fluent. The setup script narrates what it does before doing it. It stops clearly. It never overwrites without asking. The first-run experience should feel like a careful colleague setting up your workspace, not an installer running a script.

GitHub username belongs in setup.env. The two-repo architecture (public code + private canonical) requires knowing which GitHub account owns the canonical repo. This should be in setup.env from the start — GITHUB_USER and GITHUB_CANONICAL_REPO — so the setup script and Save & Break can wire both remotes automatically. Currently a manual step. Should be automatic.

The easy installer is a 2.x problem, but the friendly repo is now. Removing hardcoded paths, accurate model knowledge dates in tooltips, a setup script that narrates rather than assumes — these are not distribution features, they are respect for the researcher who will clone this repo and try to make it work. The repo should be as friendly as possible regardless of when the installer ships.

On the architecture

Sessions, references, notes, and captures are four distinct canonical types. Sessions are prompted — you asked a question, models responded. References are external — someone else's work, surfaced and verified. Notes are internal — your thinking provoked by reading, without destination. Captures are raw — OCR output awaiting your annotation. The Intelligence tab should eventually synthesise across all four, not just two. The "What am I sitting with?" synthesis mode (notes layer) is the most honest reading of what a researcher actually accumulates.

The posture slider is the right abstraction for the Socratic intent. A single control that shifts the synthesis register from Supportive (what does this material serve in my argument?) to Interrogative (where does this material resist, complicate, or undermine my argument?) — with scope selectable by project or writing piece. The writing piece as scope object lets the researcher ask "what does my library say about this specific argument" rather than the whole project. That is the unit of analysis that matters during actual writing sessions.

Scope × Posture is the Intelligence tab's final form.

Scope:    [ Project ▼ ]  →  [ Writing piece ▼ ]  →  [ All ]
Posture:  Supportive ←————————●————————→ Interrogative
Mode:     References | Sessions | Notes | Both | All
When the writing piece is selected, the framing from that piece's canonical file seeds the synthesis prompt. When the project is selected, the project framing seeds it. When all is selected, the instrument reads cold — bookstore mode, no framing injected, the collection speaks for itself.

Promoted vs transient sessions — still unbuilt. Most sessions are transient: audit trail only. Manually promoted sessions become reference records with source type: insight, field-note, capture, correspondence, working-note. These are the researcher's own knowledge production, distinct from external sources. Visual treatment difference on the card. This was an overnight idea from June 11 that didn't make it into the build. Still the right architecture.

On handwriting as friction, not nostalgia

Van der Weel & Van der Meer (2024, Frontiers in Psychology) found handwriting produces far more widespread brain connectivity than typing — 36 university students, EEG cap, digital pen vs. keyboard. The popular framing ("handwriting makes the brain work, typing lets it coast") overstates the finding — the protocol involved no actual learning task, just transcription of known words, and a formal commentary (Pinet & Longcamp, 2025) is right to caution against extrapolating to classroom learning from a lab task with no memory encoding requirement.

But the caveated version still supports something Marginalia already assumes structurally: friction is not the enemy of cognition, it may be the precondition for it. The notebook-first capture workflow — write by hand, then OCR, then deep-read, then canonical note — isn't nostalgia for paper. It's the same instinct as "slugs typed by hand, status cycled manually, Save & Break deliberate." The instrument doesn't optimize away the friction between thinking and recording. It trusts that the friction is doing something.

Cultural corroboration, June 18 2026. Hasan Minhaj Doesn't Know — "The Gen Z Luddites Rebelling Against Big Tech," S3E24 (June 17 2026) — sat down with The Luddite Club, a youth movement built around deliberately reintroducing friction: flip phones, paper books, in-person gathering, the deliberate refusal of frictionless convenience. Not a research citation, just worth noting that the instinct behind Marginalia's design — friction as a feature, not a bug to be optimized away — is showing up as an organized cultural movement among the generation that grew up with the least friction available to them. The dissertation's own student-facing argument (intellectual risk-taking, performance anxiety, embodied community-building as the antidote to frictionless atomized learning) and this youth movement are reaching for the same thing from different directions. Worth keeping in mind as a contemporary cultural reference point if the dissertation ever wants a non-academic anchor for why friction-by-design resonates beyond pedagogy theory.

On sovereign access and the case for open weights

June 2026: the US government ordered Anthropic to suspend all access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for any foreign national, inside or outside the US, including Anthropic's own non-citizen employees. Anthropic had no real-time way to verify citizenship, so it disabled the models for everyone — citizens, non-citizens, researchers mid-project, all in one stroke, with under an hour's notice and no public explanation of the underlying concern.

This is not a data-retention argument. It's sharper than that. A frontier model can be switched off by a government you don't vote for, for reasons that have nothing to do with your research, while you are mid-dissertation. No appeal, no warning, no recourse — you simply lose the tool.

The asymmetry matters most for people who are not "conventionally powerful" — not enterprise customers with account teams, not nationals of the country making the rules, not institutions with leverage to negotiate. A Canadian PhD researcher building a dissertation around community-sourced data has exactly zero standing in that conversation. The tool's availability was never actually yours to depend on.

It is worth being precise about how far this generalizes. This was not a story about individuals losing access while institutions kept theirs. Anthropic's own non-citizen employees lost access. Harvey — an enterprise legal customer with a direct vendor relationship, paying for early access — lost it in the same hour, with the same notice, as everyone else. No university, however well-funded, however deep its institutional relationship with Anthropic, could have backdoored a workaround for its students or researchers. The directive operates on nationality and jurisdiction, full stop — not on contract tier, not on grant funding, not on infrastructure. A Canadian university and a hobbyist with a free account were in the identical position that Friday evening. So would an American researcher at that same Canadian university's American partner institution, if they happened to hold the wrong passport while sitting in the wrong building.

This is the strongest argument yet for open weights, open source, and OER as more than ethical preference — they are continuity insurance. A model running on Solaris answers to nothing but the hardware and the power bill. It cannot be retracted by export control, cannot be revoked by a change in terms of service, cannot vanish because a government somewhere decided a jailbreak report warranted shutting off everyone at once. The local models in Marginalia are slower and less capable than Fable 5. They are also, in a way that now has a documented case study attached to it, durable in a way the frontier alternative is not.

"Largely local" was always partly about epistemics — different training traditions, different absences, the honest rabbit hole. After June 2026 it is also, plainly, about sovereignty in the most literal sense: who can take this away from you, and on what notice.

Addendum, June 18 2026. The case played out in real time. The US ordered Fable 5 and Mythos 5 pulled for all foreign nationals on June 12; Anthropic, unable to separate users cleanly, disabled both models worldwide. Within the same week, Cohere, Moonshot, Zhipu, and Xiaomi had open-weight coding alternatives in market or already shipped — different licenses, different countries, different hardware tiers. None of them match Fable 5 on raw capability. That was never really the point. The lesson is structural, not competitive: a team that had already qualified an alternate kept working; a team that had bet everything on one hosted frontier model lost its engine in an afternoon.

Worth resisting the instinct to chase the largest available open model as a hedge — a trillion-parameter model needing server-class multi-GPU memory is its own kind of single point of failure, just a self-hosted one instead of a cloud one. Battleships to swat bottle flies. The actual resilience in the open-weight response wasn't any one model's horsepower, it was that there were several, runnable on different machines, answerable to no single export order. Sometimes the hedge isn't a bigger gun. It's tape and a little patience — Gemma and Qwen and Mistral on a Mac Mini, modest and durable, doing the bounded task in front of them.

Open question — the slice of the pie, not the whole pie

How long until distilled local models that fit in 16GB reach Fable/Mythos/GPT-5.5 capability levels? Wrong question, or at least incomplete. Frontier capability is being marketed on long-horizon autonomous agency and open-ended frontier reasoning — the kind of thing that seems to scale with raw parameter count and training compute in ways distillation can approximate but not fully replicate. Local models on a 16GB box are much less likely to close that particular gap soon.

But that's not the capability Marginalia actually needs. OCR, synthesis, drafting, multi-voice annotation, pressure-testing a thesis statement — these are bounded, well-specified tasks, and distillation has been closing the gap on exactly this slice faster than frontier scaling has been opening it elsewhere. The real question isn't "when do local models match Fable 5" — it's "which slice of that capability does the actual workflow need," and for a dissertation research workbench, that slice may already be smaller than the marketing suggests.

The garnish that matters more than raw capability: continuity. Right now every local model fires cold — keep_alive: 0, no session memory, no sense of the line of reasoning already explored. It doesn't need to feel like a friend (explicitly not the goal — see appropriate boundaries elsewhere in this project). It needs to understand what's already been asked and answered in this session without the researcher re-supplying all the context by hand every time, the way Cohere and Mistral seemed to do mid-session on June 14 when they started "appreciating" the multi-model conversation format.

This is the actual roadmap question, not capability-per-parameter. Some kind of session-scoped context carried forward to local models — not full chat history bloating every prompt, but a lightweight working memory of what's been explored this session — closes more of the gap to frontier-feel than waiting for raw model improvement would. Tentatively: late 2.x, maybe pulled forward if it proves easier than expected. Capability catches up on its own schedule. Continuity is something Marginalia can just build.

Design sketch (doodled aloud, June 15): three layers, already mostly existing, just not yet concatenated. (1) The synthesis output from this session — already generated, currently discarded after rendering. (2) The current session's prompt/response chain — what's already been asked and answered, the thing the researcher has been doing by hand, re-pasting context each round. (3) The active project's framing statement — already exists, already injected into scoped Intelligence mode, just not yet flowing into the Prompt tab (which would also incidentally fix the phd-core-slug-missing-from-Prompt gap noted June 14).

Concatenate the three into a primer block, prepend it to the next prompt sent to local models. Short enough to feel like a long session, not a context dump. The honest framing: no model needs to remember anything. Marginalia remembers. The instrument is stateful; the models stay stateless and forgetful, exactly as keep_alive: 0 intends. That's arguably more correct than session memory living inside the model — the canonical record stays the single source of truth, not the model's own retained state.

Related but distinct — RAG over canonical (added June 18). XDA's Gemma 4 12B test (8GB GPU) surfaced a failure worth naming: given an unsolvable riddle, Gemma ground on it forever rather than recognizing the boundary — confident, ungrounded reasoning from training data alone, with no signal that it had hit a wall. The same failure mode shows up as confident invention in open factual recall (a comment thread on the same article: Gemma asked for popular Pink Floyd songs, confidently named one that doesn't exist). Small local models are not knowledgeable; they are structurally capable — good at working with text you hand them, unreliable when generating from what they "know" on their own. Source: https://www.xda-developers.com/tested-google-gemma-4-12b-on-8gb-gpu-and-dont-want-to-go-back-to-smaller-models/

The session primer above solves continuity within one sitting. It does not solve the well-falling problem, because the primer is still asking the model to reason from training data plus whatever's in this session. A proper retrieval layer would change what the model reasons about: embed the canonical record (references, notes, sessions — already markdown on disk), semantic search at prompt time, inject only the relevant retrieved chunks rather than relying on the model's own memory or the researcher manually finding and pasting the right excerpt. This is closer to what the multi-voice reference annotation already does in miniature — fetch the reference's content, then ask the model to comment on what's actually in front of it, not on what it half-remembers from training.

Worth keeping separate from the session primer in the roadmap. The primer is "what have we discussed today." RAG over canonical is "what do I already know about this, going back months." Both are v2.x territory; RAG is probably the harder build (chunking, embedding model choice, retrieval quality) but the higher-value one for actually keeping local models out of the well.

Independent confirmation, June 19 2026. XDA published a follow-up — a researcher hit the identical failure with Gemma 4 (confidently invented Computex 2026 product announcements that didn't exist) and tried to solve it two ways. First attempt: scan Gemma's output for hedging language ("I'm not sure," "I don't have access to," etc.) and escalate to Claude when detected. This failed immediately, for the exact reason predicted above — Gemma does not hedge. It completes prompts confidently regardless of whether the content is real. Detection-after-generation cannot catch a model that never signals uncertainty in the first place. Source: XDA comment thread on Gemma 4 12B article, June 19 2026 — not independently citable as a standalone article. Parent article: https://www.xda-developers.com/tested-google-gemma-4-12b-on-8gb-gpu-and-dont-want-to-go-back-to-smaller-models/

Second attempt worked: classify the query before any model sees it, not the answer after generation. Stable queries (explanations, brainstorming, writing tasks) stay local. Fact-sensitive, recency-dependent, or verification-requiring queries route to a grounded source before the prompt ever reaches the local model. Neither model needs to know the other exists — the routing decision happens in the layer above both. His framing: "Gemma just needed a co-worker, not a total replacement." That's the multi-model architecture's whole thesis, arrived at independently.

This sharpens the open question for Marginalia considerably. Synthesis already catches some of this — the "Absent Voices" and divergence cards surface where models contradict each other or where something's clearly missing — but that's a post-hoc catch, after the local model has already generated and the researcher has already read it. The XDA case proves post-hoc detection doesn't work for the failure mode that matters most: confident invention with no hedge to detect. By the time synthesis flags a gap, the researcher may have already half-trusted a fabricated detail.

The real design question, not yet answered: does Marginalia need a pre-prompt interception stage — classify the query before it reaches a local model, route fact-sensitive or citation-bearing prompts toward RAG-grounded or cloud-verified answers automatically — or is a lighter touch enough, a visible reminder at prompt-time ("this question may need grounding — local models can be confidently wrong on facts/dates/recent events") that keeps the researcher's judgment in the loop rather than the system silently deciding for them. The first is more robust but adds invisible machinery the researcher doesn't control. The second is more honest to the 60/40 principle but depends on the researcher remembering to heed it. Worth sitting with before committing either way — this is RAG-over-canonical's harder sibling question, not a separate feature.

The Rushkoff frame, added June 21 2026. Douglas Rushkoff’s Team Human argument is directly useful here: the question is not whether to use the technology, it’s whether the human remains the agent or becomes the instrument. When a model times out or returns thin output and the researcher re-prompts with the relevant reference they remember, the theoretical connection the model missed, the framing from a past session — that is the researcher operating as agent, not instrument. The canonical record is the corpus; the researcher’s judgment is the retrieval mechanism. This is not a failure mode. It is the 60/40 principle at its most literal: the model does 40% (fires, generates, sometimes fails), the researcher does 60% (reads, judges, retrieves, re-prompts with knowledge the instrument has helped them accumulate).

Automating the retrieval layer in v2.x is worth doing for coverage and speed. But it should be built with Rushkoff’s question visible: does this keep the researcher as agent, or does it start making retrieval decisions the researcher should be making? A system that silently decides which passages are relevant and injects them without the researcher knowing is edging toward instrument-mode. A system that shows what it retrieved and why, lets the researcher override, and treats the retrieval as a suggestion rather than a fact — that keeps the human in the loop at the point that matters. Design accordingly.

On the dashboard that keeps drifting

There has been a parallel artifact running in a sibling Claude thread since at least early June 2026: a self-contained PhD Thread Dashboard, hand-regenerated roughly every 15 interactions, currently at v6. Dark mode, three tabs — Open Flags, Dangling Threads, Next Moves — expandable cards, each one carrying a chip (priority/status), a source pointer back into the synthesis archive, and a short body. It already names its own intended destination in its own archive note: "Sibling thread: Marginalia (LLM) workbench — Phase 8 serves this file at localhost:5000/dashboard." That integration never happened. The file kept getting regenerated by hand instead, v6 sitting in docs/dashboard-reference-v6.html as of June 18 2026.

It is worth being honest about what this artifact actually is before building toward it. It is not session data Marginalia already holds — it is a separate working memory maintained in a different Claude conversation, manually summarized into three categories that don't map cleanly onto Marginalia's existing structures. Open Flags resembles a flagged reference or a note with unresolved status. Dangling Threads resembles unresolved questions from past sessions — the kind of thing Intelligence's "What am I missing?" mode already surfaces, just not persisted anywhere as a standing list. Next Moves resembles a task list with priority and status (Reading / Writing / Admin chips) that nothing in Marginalia currently tracks at all.

The honest integration path is not "serve this static file at a new route." It's recognizing that the dashboard is doing real work — holding the shape of where the dissertation actually stands, across sessions, in a glanceable form — and asking whether Marginalia's canonical record could generate something equivalent automatically, rather than depending on a human manually re-summarizing every 15 turns in a different tool. A flyout page is the right instinct for where this lives in the UI. What it's fed by is the open design question: hand-curated forever (keeps the researcher's judgment in the loop, stays honest, never silently goes stale the way auto-generated dashboards can), or partially derived from canonical (flagged references and notes auto-populate Open Flags; unresolved Intelligence "Absent Voices" and "Argument Weaknesses" findings auto-populate Dangling Threads; Next Moves probably has to stay hand-curated, since priority is a judgment call no model should make alone).

Don't let this drift again. It's a real piece of the practice, not a side project — see v2.x roadmap.

On Open Notebook and the closest open-source analogue

June 19 2026: XDA covered Open Notebook (github.com/lfnovo/open-notebook), an open-source self-hosted research tool built explicitly as a NotebookLM alternative. Docker-based, multi-model including local Ollama models, podcast generation, multi-source corpus. Worth knowing about and worth being precise about how it differs from Marginalia. Open Notebook is a document interrogation tool. Upload sources, chat across them, generate audio summaries. The accumulation the reviewer values is research documents continuing to grow over time rather than living in temporary chats that get forgotten. The question it answers is "what does this document say?" — bounded source corpus, retrieve and synthesize across it. The friction it removes is commercial: NotebookLM's daily limits, Google's data terms, the inability to use your own models.

Marginalia is not a document interrogation tool. The canonical record accumulates your thinking about what you've read, not the documents themselves. References, notes, sessions — the researcher's annotations, connections, unresolved questions, argument fragments — are the corpus, not the PDFs. The question Marginalia answers is "what do you think, and can you defend it?" The friction it preserves is epistemological: slugs typed by hand, status cycled manually, Save & Break deliberate, the 60/40 principle that keeps the thinking irreducibly yours.

Both remove commercial/cloud friction. Only one removes epistemological friction, and that one is Open Notebook. Marginalia keeps the epistemological friction because it believes that friction is doing something. That's the distinction the July blog post needs to draw clearly — not as a criticism of Open Notebook, which is genuinely good at what it does, but as a precise description of two different theories of what a research instrument is for.

One other note: Open Notebook's podcast generation feature is interesting and Marginalia has no equivalent. Audio synthesis of your canonical record — notes and sessions read back as a structured conversation — is worth holding as a potential future feature, not for polish but because there's a genuine use case in reviewing accumulated thinking while doing something else (commuting, cooking, walking). Not soon. But not never.

On federated ‘current’ and the finger protocol (3.x)

Doodled June 21 2026 while listening to Decoder. The PhD Thread Dashboard (‘current’) is already a shareable artifact — it’s a self-contained HTML file that describes open flags, dangling threads, and next moves in a readable form. The 3.x thought: make it optionally public and queryable, federated in the old Unix ‘finger’ sense.

The finger protocol (RFC 742, 1977) let you query a remote host and see what a user was currently working on — their plan file, their project, their last login. Public by default, opt-in depth, no algorithm, no feed, no platform. Just: here is what I am thinking about right now, available to anyone who asks. The academic version of an open office door.

For Marginalia this would mean: a researcher opts in to publishing their ‘current’ at a known endpoint on their Solaris (or equivalent) instance. Another researcher’s Marginalia can query it, read it, and display it — no central platform, no subscription, no feed algorithm deciding what gets seen. Peer to peer, researcher to researcher, the way hallway conversation used to work before it moved to Twitter and then nowhere.

Federation would mean two Marginalia instances subscribing to each other’s ‘current’ without any intermediary. The sovereignty argument applies: federated sharing belongs to no platform, can’t be taken down by an export order, doesn’t require anyone’s continued goodwill. The thing you’re working on lives on your hardware and gets shared on your terms.

This is genuinely 3.x — it requires stable instances, stable endpoints, and a user base that actually runs Marginalia persistently, none of which exist yet. But the instinct is right and worth holding: the research community Marginalia could eventually support is one where the dashboard isn’t just a private instrument but a low-friction signal of intellectual activity, visible to peers who care, invisible to everyone else. Not a social network. A field note left on a corkboard that colleagues can read if they walk past.

On sequential model conditioning — "shower thought mode"

Surfaced June 21 2026 across two threads. The current Marginalia architecture fires all models in parallel against the same prompt — independent responses, synthesis reconciles them after the fact. Each model sees only the original prompt, nothing the others said.

The shower thought mode inverts this: model 1 responds, model 2 sees model 1’s response plus the original prompt, model 3 sees both prior responses plus the prompt, and so on. The final output carries the accumulated conditioning of the whole chain. Not a synthesis of independent voices — a thought that has been through several minds in sequence, each one leaving a mark.

The interesting property is that each model brings its own training bias to the prior response. Cohere might tighten a loose argument. Mistral might push back on an assumption Cohere accepted. DeepSeek might surface a connection neither reached. The chain doesn’t reconcile differences, it propagates and transforms them. The output is genuinely different from anything the parallel architecture produces.

Best suited to generative, associative, exploratory thinking — following a thread somewhere unexpected, the loose reasoning that happens when you’re not trying to land anywhere specific. Not suited to tight analytical work where error propagation matters: if model 1 makes a confident wrong turn, models 2 and 3 may build on it rather than correct it. The current parallel architecture is immune to this by design. Sequential mode trades that immunity for depth. Worth surfacing this trade-off clearly in the UI when the mode is selected.

Late 2.x placement, and genuinely so — not just because the architecture is a departure but because the mode won’t work well until the instrument has been used enough to have real material to feed it. Without canonical depth, model 1 generates generic content and models 2 and 3 just polish it. The interesting version is when model 1 is already working against a grounded prompt carrying session context, canonical references, and project framing — then the chain refines something with substance rather than amplifying noise. Dependency chain: session primer → canonical depth → shower thought mode. It’s secondary, not primary, and should never be the default firing mode. The name "shower thought mode" is good and should probably stay — it sets accurate expectations about what kind of thinking this is for.

On the cognitive agent and unrealized capital

Unrealized cognitive capital. Satya Nadella's framing from Hard Fork (June 2026): unrealized capital — productive capacity that exists but isn't being deployed. The parallel for research: unrealized cognitive capital. The knowledge a researcher has built through years of reading, teaching, coaching, and living — held in their head, in their margins, in the silence between sessions — is not accessible to the tools they use. Marginalia is an attempt to make that capital legible without extracting it. The canonical files are the ledger. The instrument is the accountant that works for the researcher, not the institution.

The right fit model is part of this. Not everything needs frontier. DeepSeek R1 on your dissertation literature is more useful than GPT-4o on everything, because it is scoped. The locally-held, community-specific, epistemologically-situated knowledge is often more valuable than the globally-averaged one. This is the same argument the dissertation makes about first-generation students. The instrument is the proof of concept.

The cognitive agent as collaborative partner — not tool. Nadella talks about coding where there needs to be a cognitive agent. Marginalia was built in exactly this mode. The researcher held the epistemological commitments and the domain knowledge — use cases as feelings, a sense of what research actually is, the hockey metaphors, the decolonial frame, the ethics board implications. The agent held the implementation knowledge. The interface between them was natural language and trust built over a week of sessions.

This is not "AI writes code." Not "human directs AI." Something more like: the researcher reads the ice, calls the play, the agent executes. A week of sessions produced a working research instrument with Notes tab, Intelligence tab, OCR capture pipeline, six local models, section card rendering, the boulder concept, and the Socratic posture design. A solo developer who already knew the stack would have taken 6-8 weeks on the code alone. The design decisions — Absent Voices, the 60/40 principle, the Canadian chip, the ethics board schema — those would have taken longer or not arrived at all.

The interesting question Nadella doesn't answer: what happens to the knowledge built in that collaboration? The Seeds doc is part of the answer. The canonical sessions are part of the answer. The instrument remembers what we decided and why, even across threads. That is unrealized cognitive capital made legible — not just the code, but the reasoning behind it.

Pinpoint addendum — the pipeline question. Pinpoint is moving toward Google Docs integration — ingest, analyse, write, all within Google's ecosystem. Seamless but enclosed. The pipeline exists in Marginalia too: ingest → references → notes → sessions → writing. The difference is ownership. "The pipeline exists in both. The question is who owns the data at rest."

Session files as training data and agents — 2.x territory. Session canonical files are structured enough to become: fine-tuning data for a local model trained on the researcher's specific domain and reasoning patterns; RAG context for a local agent that has read everything the researcher has thought about; longitudinal analysis of argument development over months. The ethics board dimension: if community conversations are in the session files, they become training data. The provenance fields — authors, capture_context, your role — are the consent and attribution layer. Build it right from the start. The architecture already supports it.

On the mischief problem

Marginalia grins at a field. Code wants a fence. Code is deterministic. It wants inputs, outputs, defined states, clean transitions. if research_question: run_synthesis(). Research wanders into a footnote and finds the real question. It follows a hunch that doesn't have a field in any schema. It sits with something for three weeks before knowing what it is. Marginalia is trying to hold that wandering in a structure that code can touch. Every design decision is a negotiation between the mischief and the machine.

The risk is that the code wins. That every new feature adds another dropdown, another status field, another workflow. That the instrument slowly becomes a very elegant rows-and-rows system with a nice font. The boulder, the Notes tab with no status workflow, the session note prompt that just asks "where are you?", the Socratic posture — these are deliberate resistances to the if/then. They need to be named and protected as the instrument grows.

The canonical files are the compromise. Plain markdown, human-readable, flexible enough to hold a half-formed thought, structured enough that Flask can parse the frontmatter. The format was chosen so the instrument can touch the artifacts without owning them. A researcher can open any canonical file in a text editor and read it, edit it, move it. The code is a guest in the researcher's files, not the landlord.

Software brain — attribution and correct framing. Software brain is Nilay Patel's concept, developed across multiple Vergecast episodes and a dedicated Decoder episode (April 2026). His framing: software people are trained to think everything is a software problem — automation is always a win, LLMs are the best automation tools in decades. Most people don't think this way. They see bullshit features, jobs disappearing, energy going to data centres. Software brain is the cognitive formation that makes automation feel like progress regardless of what it costs. Source: https://www.theverge.com/podcast/917029/software-brain-ai-backlash-databases-automation

The concept arrived in Marginalia's design vocabulary through this framing — not as an original concept but as an external lens that named something the tool was already being built against. The design brief (slow down, largely local, extrusive not extractive, intentional friction) preceded the name. Patel gave the name. The fit was immediate.

Why software brain resists rows — and why that matters. Software brain is the reference card format. It would be perfectly happy filed as Patel_2026_SoftwareBrain.md with a verification status and a completion state — that is exactly what it does to everything. Filing it that way would be an instance of the very thing it names. It's a lens, not a reference. The correct canonical treatment is to use it as a prompt you bring to synthesis — look through Marginalia at what's already in the canon and see what's swimming in the software brain light. Zuboff is there. Doctorow is there. The student who says "just tell me what to do" is there. The concept doesn't need a file. It needs a question.

The process resists if/then. The instrument should too. Code doesn't like rabbit holes. Marginalia looks at a field and starts grinning. The design challenge for every future feature: does this add a fence or does this open a gate? Dropdowns add fences. The Notes tab opens a gate. The boulder opens a gate. The Socratic posture opens a gate. When in doubt, open the gate.

Correction — software brain is a lens, not a container

Extrusive, not extractive. Pinpoint and systems like it are extractive — ingest documents, pull out information, surface what's there. Marginalia is extrusive — the knowledge lives on paper notes, in scattered writing, in the feeling in your chest when a student says "just tell me what to do." The movement is reversed. Not pulling information in and processing it. Pushing knowledge outward from the researcher's scattered thinking through the lens of the instrument.

Software brain is quanta, not content. Software brain is not a thing to be ingested. It's a lens. You don't put software brain into Marginalia — you look through Marginalia at what's already there and software brain is what you see swimming. Zuboff wasn't filed under software brain. But looked at through that lens, she's swimming right there. The concept doesn't need a canonical file. It needs a prompt. A question you bring to the instrument, not a container you fill.

The Intelligence tab is a lens grinder, not a search engine. The writing piece as scope doesn't mean "show me everything tagged software brain." It means "look at what's here through this specific angle and tell me what you see." Same water, different light. The posture slider doesn't change what's in the collection. It changes what the researcher is looking for. This is a stronger argument for the posture slider than any efficiency argument — it's about epistemological stance, not retrieval scope.

The concept file idea was wrong. The earlier proposal for a concept canonical type — a named idea that accumulates evidence — imports the extractive logic. It wants to file software brain, give it a status, make it a row. Software brain resists this. It is always becoming, never filed. The right instrument holds it as a question that can be brought to any synthesis, not an entity with a completion state. Delete the concept file from the roadmap. Keep the lens.

"Look through the lens and see what's swimming about." This is the design brief for the Intelligence tab's final form. Not a dashboard. Not a retrieval system. A place to bring a question to your own accumulated thinking and see what surfaces. The posture slider, the scope selector, the mode buttons — these are all ways of adjusting the lens, not ways of searching the collection.

The most important correction — Marginalia is built against software brain

Software brain is what Marginalia resists, not what it studies. Software brain = extractive, automatic, thinks in databases, optimizes for throughput. Marginalia is the counter-argument made operational. The instrument was built by someone who has spent years watching software brain happen to students — the erosion of the capacity to sit with uncertainty, to hold a question without immediately resolving it, to let thinking be slow and non-linear. Marginalia is not a study of that erosion. It is a refusal of it.

The slugs are pins in the corkboard. A dropdown that auto-populates and auto-links is software brain. A slug you type manually is a pin in the corkboard. You hold the thread in your hand for a moment. You decide what connects to what. The friction is the thinking. This was never an implementation detail — it was a design principle that was present before it was named.

No real database. Plain text. Intentional. A database optimizes for retrieval. Markdown optimizes for legibility. The canonical files are human-readable, editable with any tool, ownable by the researcher. The choice was never about technology — it was about who the files belong to and what kind of thinking they invite. Software brain would have used SQLite for everything and called it efficient. Marginalia uses SQLite only where necessary and plain markdown everywhere it can.

The intentional friction is the argument. Every deliberate resistance in Marginalia is a small refusal of software brain:

Slugs typed by hand — you decide what connects to what
Status cycled manually — surfaced → located → verified is a reading practice, not a metadata field
Save & Break as a deliberate gesture — not autosave
The 2h prompt — not a timer, an interruption that asks where you are
The boulder — not a deadline, a presence that grows heavier
Notes with no status workflow — accumulation without resolution
The Socratic synthesis — questions, not answers
The posture slider — you choose the angle, the instrument doesn't assume one
These are not UX decisions. They are epistemological commitments made visible in the interface.

The instrument knows what it's pushing back against. That knowledge came from the researcher, not from the code. The code would have been perfectly happy to build another Pinpoint. The design brief — slow down, largely local, extrusive not extractive, the boulder never crests — came from someone who has watched software brain colonize classrooms and wanted to build something that thinks differently about thinking.

This belongs in the dissertation. Not as a footnote about the tool used for the research. As evidence that the researcher understood the problem from the inside well enough to build its opposite.

On re-entry and the side door (late 2.x / early 3.x)

The re-entry document. Current is not just a live dashboard — it is a re-entry document. When life creates a gap (a week, a semester, a year), the canonical record should be able to answer three questions cleanly: where you were, what got you there, and where you were pointed. Not a todo list. A reminder that the interest was real and that there is an entry point that doesn't require picking up the heaviest thing first.

Three panels:

Where you were — last few sessions, open questions that hadn't closed, writing pieces mid-draft
What got you there — references active in that window, synthesis outputs that landed, annotations that were fresh
Where you were pointed — seeds, flagged open questions, "still open" Pressure Test items, writing pieces with no draft yet. Second star to the right.
The time window is not a setting — it's a read. Marginalia looks at the canonical record and finds the natural cluster of activity before the gap. Sessions have timestamps. The record of when things were alive is already there. The re-entry document generates from the activity boundary, not an arbitrary 30-day window.

The side door. Some days the dissertation chapter isn't available. But a reference annotation is. A seed thought. A single open question that's been sitting. The re-entry document surfaces the lowest-friction entry point available, not the highest-obligation one. "I don't want to work on chapter three today — what else is alive?" Momentum doesn't always look like forward motion on the main thing. Marginalia surfaces the side door.

As a modal within Intelligence. Gap detection: read created_at across sessions chronologically, find where delta between consecutive sessions exceeds threshold (configurable — one week default), take everything before that gap as the last active window. Three or four filesystem reads, some date math, a template render. No model required — pure canonical archaeology. The modal only appears if a gap over threshold is detected. Invisible when active, present when needed.

Lazy load everything — the modal doesn't fetch until opened. Intelligence tab should stay fast on open, expensive on demand.

On the Files / Intelligence split (2.x)

The right axis is physical vs cognitive. As Intelligence grows it will need to split. The correct axis is not by feature — it's by epistemic type.

Files tab — archival, deterministic, yours. Sessions, references, notes, writing pieces as a canonical browser. What exists. Timestamped, browseable, patchable. The re-entry document lives here because it reads the record without interpreting it. No model required for any of it. This is the 60.

Intelligence tab — emergent, interpretive, model-dependent. Synthesis, Pressure Test, What am I missing, the posture slider. What the record means when a model reads across it. Always derived, never canonical truth. This is the 40.

The distinction maps onto the tool's core principle directly. You don't want archival and interpretive in the same place because they represent different epistemic moves — tending the record vs. thinking with it.

Re-entry sits on the boundary deliberately. The gap detection and three panels are pure file reading — archival. But there is an optional model pass available: given what was alive then, what is the lowest friction entry point now. So re-entry lives in Files but carries an optional Intelligence layer on top, same pattern as Capture — pdfplumber is archival, Gemma OCR is emergent.

The Files tab probably comes into existence when the session list outgrows Intelligence. That is not far off. Not a v1.x problem, but worth naming before it gets built around.

On the posture slider and the seed of bias

The slider cannot touch the prompt. If the posture slider modifies the prompt before models see it, the instrument has already leaned before the researcher has heard the response. That is not posture — that is steering. The raw responses must be clean.

The slider operates on the synthesis pass only — after raw model responses come back unmodified. The researcher hears what the models said first, then declares: now synthesise this supportively, or now interrogate it. Posture is a declared interpretive lens applied after the fact, not a pre-digested question.

This means the slider lives next to the synthesis model selector, not near the prompt textarea. It already has a home. The researcher chooses posture after seeing the landscape, not before entering it.

The slider should also be available in Intelligence synthesis — same logic. You have read your sessions; now do you want the pass to be supportive or interrogative. Same control, two application points, both post-raw-response. Never touches the prompt in either location.

On backup/packout and restore (v1.99x → v2.0.0x)

1.99x — backup and packout. Before the 2.0 migration, Marginalia needs a "pack out" function: archive the entire canonical record into a portable, human-readable zip. This is the pre-flight for researchers moving from the v1.x geek-deploy to the v2.0 Tauri app. It also serves as a general backup gesture — the researcher owns the files, and this makes that ownership portable.

Backup format — no proprietary format, ever:

marginalia-backup-[YYYYMMDD]-[slug].zip
  canonical/
    references/
    sessions/
    notes/
    projects/
    writing/
  setup.env.template      # keys stripped, structure preserved
  backup-manifest.md      # human-readable: what's here, when made, version it came from
The manifest is the critical piece. An opaque zip five years from now is useless. A zip with a manifest is a research record. The manifest lists: backup date, Marginalia version, counts (N references, N sessions, N notes, N projects, N writing pieces), and a short note field the researcher can fill before packing out.

2.0.0x — restore from backup. The first-run installer offers two paths: fresh start, or import existing canonical record. Import unzips the backup, validates the manifest, places the canonical folder, and opens Marginalia with the researcher's full record intact. The canonical record travels — your research doesn't live in the tool, it lives in the files, and the files move with you.

This is the sovereignty argument made concrete. A researcher who's been running Marginalia since v1.x on a Mac Mini can pack out, install the Tauri app on any machine, restore, and continue. No data migration, no export format, no intermediary. Just a zip that a human can open with any archive tool even if Marginalia ceases to exist.

On model host extensibility (v2.1–2.5)

The current model surface: Marginalia speaks three protocols: Ollama (local), OpenAI-compatible (GPT-4o), Anthropic native, Gemini native. That covers most researchers but misses the technical audience who are already running inference infrastructure.

Target hosts for technical users:

LM Studio — local models, OpenAI-compatible API, different model enumeration endpoint. Almost wired already — OLLAMA_HOST pointing at LM Studio mostly works, small adapter needed.
llama.cpp server — purist option. Raw, fast, no GUI. Same OpenAI-compatible surface.
vLLM — what hardcore CS researchers actually run. GPU clusters, batched inference, production throughput. OpenAI-compatible but model listing and keep_alive semantics differ.
Hugging Face Inference Endpoints — cloud inference without OpenAI/Anthropic. Increasingly common in academic settings.
The right architecture — OpenAI-compatible endpoint registry: Not a new adapter per host. A MODEL_HOSTS config in setup.env accepting a list of OpenAI-compatible endpoints: name, URL, auth token. The chip in the UI is dynamic — a vLLM server at https://gpu.lab.ualberta.ca shows up as a chip the same way a local Ollama model does. Researcher adds endpoint, models enumerate, chip appears.

⚠ DIFFICULT TO TEST flag. This feature cannot ship until there is a way to validate it that doesn't require standing up a vLLM cluster between marking sessions. By v2.1–2.5, meat-space life (marking, homework, hockey) is fully active alongside dissertation work. Two options before this ships:

A mock OpenAI-compatible endpoint that runs locally and mimics the API surface for CI purposes.
A trusted external tester who actually runs vLLM or LM Studio infrastructure.
Neither exists yet. The flag holds until one does. Do not let enthusiasm for the feature override the test constraint — a broken endpoint registry is worse than no endpoint registry, because it silently fails mid-session when a researcher is depending on it.

Placement: v2.1 if mock endpoint is built alongside. v2.5 if it waits for external testers. Not before.

On open source community, error reporting, and contribution (v1.99x → 2.0)

The maintenance model changes at 2.0. Right now: one researcher, one Mac Mini, known hardware, known models, controlled environment. After 2.0: unknown machines, unknown Ollama versions, unknown network configs, unknown OS versions, researchers who are not Raj. Open source means building with best intentions and good practice — but not being able to predict what other systems will do. Some hope that others will bring fixes is part of the model. That has to be named honestly, not buried in fine print.

Error reporting — "send this to the team": When something breaks, the researcher needs a one-click way to package the error and send it somewhere useful. Not telemetry, not silent logging — a deliberate gesture, consistent with everything Marginalia believes about researcher agency. The researcher sees the error, decides to report it, sends it.

What gets sent: error message, Marginalia version, OS, active model, nothing else. No canonical data, no prompts, no references. Privacy by design, not by policy.

The mechanism: a button opens a GitHub issue URL with the error pre-encoded in the issue body. No server required on Marginalia's end. The researcher needs a GitHub account to submit — a reasonable bar for the 2.0 technical audience. The issue gets a number. The fix references the number. The broadcast banner release notes reference the number. "v2.1.3 — fixes #142, #156, #160 — vLLM cold start, OCR timeout on multi-page scan, Intel session list refresh." Researchers see that reports matter and things get fixed.

CONTRIBUTING.md — a 1.99x deliverable: Not long. Honest. What the project is, what it's for, how to report a bug, how to propose a feature, what kinds of PRs are likely to be accepted. The last point matters and should be said plainly: a PR that adds telemetry, removes intentional friction, or moves toward extractive design is not consistent with what Marginalia is. Contributors should understand the project before spending time on something that won't land. This is respect for contributors, not gatekeeping.

Contributions — named or anonymous, contributor's choice: A CONTRIBUTORS file (or section in README) that lists everyone who has contributed code, bug reports, testing, ideas, or feedback. Named or anonymous — the contributor decides, not the project. Anonymous contributions are listed as "Anonymous" with the nature of the contribution noted if the contributor permits even that.

The arts donation model: the work matters, the credit is the contributor's to give or withhold.

Anonymous contribution is in the tradition of the original author of this software, inspired by MASH. In a Christmas episode (S9E10) Charles provided a great gift to the local orphans, but is then frustrated when his generous, but inappropriate gift is used to support the children with basic needs. He feels that his tradition was violated, but when Klinger, the company clerk, finds out that Charles is not feeling that his gift was appreciated, he pays back the anonymous gesture with a meal and a simple gift — on the promise that the source remains anonymous.

It is not the size of the gift that matters, but the spirit with which it is given. People great and small, known and unknown, working to help their community is what is celebrated.

Marginalia holds anonymous contribution the same way. Not a checkbox, not an accommodation — a tradition. The project shouldn't replicate the institutional failure of demanding credit on the institution's terms. If someone fixes a bug at midnight between marking sessions and wants no credit, that is honored completely and without question. The work is in the canonical record regardless. The name is theirs to give or withhold.

This applies to bug reporters too, not just code contributors. Someone who finds and clearly documents a crash on a fresh Ubuntu install has contributed something real. They get listed if they want to be.

The community Marginalia could eventually support is researchers who understand what the tool is pushing back against. That's a small, self-selecting group — and that's fine. Better a small community that understands the project than a large one that doesn't.

OpenFugu, shower thought mode, and the routing intuition (June 2026)

The connection Raj noticed: OpenFugu feels like shower thought mode. This is correct and not naive.

Shower thought mode (Marginalia seeds, earlier): sequential model conditioning — one model's output becomes context for the next rather than all models firing in parallel against the same prompt. The second model doesn't just answer the question, it responds to how the first model answered it. The chain is visible to the researcher. The seams are the point.

OpenFugu/Fugu: a ~0.6B coordinator reads the query, routes to the single best worker from a pool, returns that one answer. The routing is learned (sep-CMA-ES on ~19.5K parameters), not rule-based. The coordinator is trained to know which model is best positioned for which query type. The seams are deliberately hidden — "one model to command them all."

The convergence: both are saying that parallel independent firing is not always the right shape for a query. Sometimes sequence matters. Sometimes one model should inform another. Sometimes routing matters more than breadth. Same instinct, different implementations, different philosophical commitments about transparency.

Where they diverge — and why it matters for Marginalia:

Fugu: efficiency + capability. One good answer fast. Routing is the product.
Shower thought mode: epistemological transparency. The chain is visible. Divergence is the point.
Marginalia will never hide the seams. The researcher sees which model said what and where they disagreed. That's not a limitation — it's the argument.

Mills (2023) — Cultural Representation in AI — empirical backing for multi-model diversity

Full citation (preprint — verify published version before citing): Atari, M., Xue, M. J., Park, P. S., Blasi, D. E., & Henrich, J. (2023). Which humans? Department of Human Evolutionary Biology, Harvard University. Corresponding author: Mohammad Atari, UMass Amherst (matari@umass.edu). PDF in canonical references. Check Google Scholar for published journal version before citing in dissertation. Adapted figure circulated by Center for Behavioral Decisions (LinkedIn, June 2026) — that's where it surfaced, not the original source.

The finding: GPT's responses cluster most closely with WEIRD populations (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) — specifically US, Canada, UK, Australia, Germany, Netherlands. Cultural similarity to GPT decreases as cultural distance from the US increases. Tested against 94,000+ respondents across 65 countries using the World Values Survey.

Why it matters for Marginalia: This is the empirical case for what Marginalia's chip row is already doing structurally. Mistral trained on European data, DeepSeek on Chinese data, Qwen on Asian/global south data, Command R7B on Canadian data — firing all of them isn't just about getting more answers, it's about getting responses shaped by different value systems, different knowledge geographies, different epistemological defaults. The "Absent Voices" section in Intelligence synthesis exists precisely because of this.

For the dissertation: The argument that first-generation and "new to academy" students bring valid knowledge systems that institutions misread as deficits maps directly onto this finding. Western-default AI in the classroom is another institutional layer of the same bias — the tool the instructor reaches for carries the same WEIRD skew as the curriculum it's meant to support. A researcher who knows this and builds against it is making a methodological commitment, not just a technical one.

Additional findings from full paper — for close reading:

WEIRD in, WEIRD out — their framing, directly quotable. The stochastic parrot amendment: not just a parrot, but one trained on WEIRD data, so its outputs "sound bizarre and outlandish to billions of people living in less-WEIRD populations."

Self-concept finding — most directly useful for the classroom argument. When asked to describe an average person, GPT responds with personal attributes ("I am athletic, I am hard-working") — the WEIRD individualist self-concept. Less-WEIRD populations describe themselves through social roles and kin relations. When GPT models "the student" in an educational context, it's modelling a WEIRD student with a WEIRD self-concept. That is not neutral. That is a design choice with consequences.

Thinking style finding — GPT thinks analytically (abstract categorization) rather than holistically (contextual relationships). Analytic vs holistic thinking maps directly onto the Western/Cartesian institutional defaults the dissertation is pushing against. GPT clusters closest to Netherlands, Finland, Sweden, Ireland on this measure — not even US-default, but hyper-analytic Northern European default.

The correlation — r = -.70 between cultural distance from the US and GPT-human similarity. Strong, robust across three methods. Not a subtle effect.

Important caveat from the authors — larger models will not necessarily reduce WEIRD bias. It is not about size, it is about the diversity of training data. Multilingual LLMs still behave WEIRDly even when responding in non-English languages (Havaldar et al., 2023, cited in paper).

Reference enrichment system — annotation, connections, writing harvest (next builds)

The core insight: Annotation on import removes onboarding friction but that's not the end state. The machine gives you a starting gun, not a finishing line. "WhoNWho (20XX) said blorg" on import — useful. Then the researcher thinks on it and adds: "sure, but there is also the other thing it doesn't seem to grok, but has a solid view of blorp." The machine annotation is the scaffold. The human annotation is the building.

Two-layer annotation field:

Machine layer — TLDR generated on import or on demand. Structured output: one or two declarative sentences. Timestamped ("Generated by Gemma + DeepSeek, June 24 2026"). Read-only unless the researcher edits it. Visible above the human layer as a prompt, not a replacement.

Human layer — blank by default, cursor waiting. The researcher writes what the machine missed, what it got wrong, what surprised them, what connection it couldn't see because it hasn't read the other 73 references the way the researcher has. The thing that came from reading Battiste at 11pm after a session on Freire. The connection the machine would never make because it wasn't in the room.

Synthesis uses both layers. Machine layer gives breadth — conceptual overlaps across 74 references quickly. Human layer gives depth — carries the insight only the researcher knows.

This is the 60/40 principle applied to the reference library. The machine does the surface reading. The researcher does the thinking.

Connections field: Generated alongside the TLDR — "this reference shares conceptual territory with: Freire (1970), Cajete (1994), Battiste (2015)." Machine proposes from the existing canonical library. Researcher confirms, adds, removes. The connection list feeds Intelligence synthesis directly — when a prompt touches Battiste, synthesis can ask "what else in your library is Battiste in conversation with?"

Batch annotation: "Annotate all unannotated" button — queues all references with empty annotation through the multi-voice pipeline sequentially. RIS/BibTeX import should offer this automatically on completion: "74 references imported — annotate all now or later?" Come back after a run. The annotations won't be perfect but they'll be enough for synthesis to find connections. Researcher corrects the ones that matter, leaves the rest as the machine's best guess.

Writing file harvest: Drop a PDF or document into a writing piece and the system scans it for citations — anything matching a reference already in canonical gets flagged as a connection automatically. Anything that doesn't match gets surfaced as a potential new reference stub with status "surfaced." Closes the loop between active writing and the reference library without manual linking. Same pattern as the leads panel from the Prompt tab — machine surfaces candidates, researcher confirms.

Placement: Single reference annotation button already exists. Next build adds structured TLDR + connections output format. Batch annotation is a separate button on the References tab header. Writing file harvest is a new drop zone in the Writing tab. All three share the same backend annotation pipeline — different triggers, same output structure.

Canonical-informed search signal (v1.7) — search as boundary probe not answer source

The inversion that matters: Not "I don't know something → search → get answer → trust it." But "I have a body of work → canonical record informs the query → search finds what's adjacent to what I already know → surface genuine gaps."

The canonical record goes first. The search query is shaped by what's already there. "I have Battiste, Cajete, Freire, Merleau-Ponty, Varela in this cluster — find me what's in conversation with this constellation that I don't have yet." Search isn't filling ignorance, it's mapping the edges of knowledge already built.

Two entry points, one mechanism. This spec was originally written (June 25) as project-driven: open a project, the canonical references attached to it shape the query. A second, equally valid entry point surfaced June 30 from a Pressure Test gap-check (see "Consensus and concept-adjacent discovery" entry above): when Prompt Pressure Test flags an UNASKED angle, fire the same search-signal mechanism using the flagged angle as the query instead of project-derived terms. Both are the same underlying feature — canonical-aware search, leads-only output, researcher decides — just triggered from two different moments in the workflow. Build the mechanism once, wire both triggers to it.

The flow (project-driven trigger):

Researcher opens a project or fires a prompt
Marginalia reads canonical references connected to that project
Extracts author names, key terms from TLDRs and annotations (requires v1.5/1.6 annotation enrichment first)
Constructs search query from the gaps — "related to [terms] but NOT [authors already present]"
Returns results as leads only — "these authors/titles appeared, none are in your library yet"
Researcher decides which leads to chase via DOI lookup
The flow (Pressure Test trigger):

Researcher runs Prompt Pressure Test on a question before firing synthesis
Pressure Test flags an UNASKED angle from the model's own reasoning
Same search-signal mechanism fires, using the flagged angle as query terms instead of project-derived terms
Returns as a quick addendum on the Pressure Test output — "here's what exists on this, if anything"
Researcher decides whether to chase any lead via DOI lookup, same gate as the project-driven path
The "NOT already in your library" exclusion is the key move. Not surfacing what you know. Surfacing what's adjacent to what you know that you don't have yet.

Why this needs v1.6 first (project-driven path): Before batch annotation exists the project-driven query has nothing to work from. The canonical density (74+ references with TLDRs and connections) is the prerequisite for that entry point specifically. The Pressure Test trigger doesn't have this dependency — it can ship as soon as the underlying search mechanism exists, since its query terms come from the flagged angle, not from canonical density.

Web search solves the temporal problem. Marginalia solves the epistemological one. A Brave-search-augmented local model still reasons from its training defaults — it just knows what happened last week. A model with your annotated Battiste note in context is more reliably grounded than one that searched "Battiste Indigenous pedagogy" and got three abstract snippets. The researcher's canonical record is the grounding layer. Search is a boundary probe. These are different jobs.

Implementation note (June 30): the underlying search call doesn't need DDG or Brave specifically — academic_sources.py (shipped v1.6.0) already provides exactly this capability via Semantic Scholar/OpenAlex fetch_paper(query=...). Both trigger points can call the existing module directly. No new API integration required; this is wiring, not a new subsystem. This is Option 1 of the three-stage concept-discovery resolution path (see entry above) — the cheapest, most immediately buildable stage, and it satisfies this v1.7 spec directly.

Placement: v1.7 — after bulk annotation (v1.6, shipped) and the academic sources integration (v1.6.0–v1.6.3, shipped). Both prerequisites are now satisfied. This is ready to build. Needs at least one feature bump of testing before 2.0 to be stable.

API options: academic_sources.py's existing Semantic Scholar/OpenAlex integration, not DDG or Brave as originally scoped — the module already exists and does this job. The query result is discarded after the canonical matching pass for the project-driven trigger; for the Pressure Test trigger, results display directly as the addendum and only enter canonical if the researcher explicitly saves one. Nothing from a search enters the prompt context or canonical record automatically in either path.

Shower thought mode — design, coding, and pedagogical applications (June 28 2026)

The run observation: shower thought mode is a natural shape for design and coding outputs, not just research synthesis. And by extension — lesson plans, course elements, pedagogical design.

Why the sequential shape fits design better than parallel:

In research you want parallel firing — all models responding independently to the same question, divergence is the signal. In design you want sequential conditioning — model A proposes, model B critiques knowing what A said, model C refines based on both. That's how a good design review actually works. Nobody in a design meeting responds as if they haven't heard the previous speaker.

The shower thought mode for coding/design:

DeepSeek proposes the architecture — good at reasoning through technical problems step by step
Mistral reviews it — different training, catches assumptions DeepSeek baked in
Qwen refines — particularly good at implementation details
Command R7B grounds it — "will this actually work in practice"
Each model responds to the accumulated thinking of the ones before it, not to the original prompt cold. The output is distilled through four passes. For a design decision that's a more useful shape than four independent answers.

The epistemic distinction from Pressure Test: Pressure Test takes parallel responses and asks "what survived, what was destabilized." Shower thought mode never produces parallel responses — it produces a chain. You're not comparing four answers, you're watching an idea develop through four minds. Different move, different output, different use case.

The pedagogical application: Lesson plans and course elements are design problems. The same sequential conditioning applies:

DeepSeek: what are the learning objectives and how do they sequence?
Mistral: what does the European pedagogical tradition suggest about this approach?
Qwen: what cultural contexts does this lesson assume, what's missing?
Command R7B: what does Canadian curriculum context require?
Gemma: what does the research literature say about this approach?
The final output isn't any one model's lesson plan — it's a plan that has been proposed, critiqued, culturally checked, contextually grounded, and research-validated in sequence. That's a richer pedagogical design process than a single model generating a lesson plan from scratch.

The WEIRD check built in: Because the models come from different training geographies, the sequential pass is also an implicit WEIRD audit. Mistral will notice European assumptions in DeepSeek's proposal. Qwen will notice assumptions about student familiarity with Western academic norms. The chain of conditioning surfaces the cultural specificity of each model's defaults in a way that parallel firing only shows in the divergence section of synthesis.

For Marginalia's build queue: Shower thought mode needs a "target output" selector when it's configured — Research synthesis (parallel) or Design/pedagogical (sequential chain). The chain order matters and should be researcher-controlled. A drag-to-reorder chip sequence for shower thought mode is the right UI gesture — the researcher decides which voice goes first and why.

Placement: v2.x — needs the multi-turn /api/chat architecture that MCP tool calling also requires. Not v1.x. But worth designing now before the architecture decision gets made.

Three synthesis modes — Survey, Shower Thought, Pressure Test (v1.5)

The three modes named cleanly (June 28 2026):

Survey — parallel firing, all voices independent, find the divergence. The wide lens. What does the landscape look like? Current default mode.

Shower Thought — sequential conditioning, each model builds on the last, distilled output. The focused lens. How does an idea develop through multiple minds? Generative, moves forward. v2.x (needs /api/chat multi-turn architecture).

Pressure Test — adversarial examination. The hard lens. What breaks? What doesn't? What was assumed? Two application points, same mode, different targets:

From the hop (before firing): the prompt itself goes under scrutiny. Is this a good question? Is it loaded? What assumptions are baked into the framing? What am I not asking?
After synthesis: does this argument hold? Survived · Destabilized · Still Open.
Prompt pressure test vs synthesis pressure test: Synthesis pressure test: "does this argument hold." Prompt pressure test: "is this the right argument to be making." One examines output. One examines the question. Both are interrogative. Both are rigorous. Different targets, same mode.

The workflow with all three: Prompt → Survey (wide lens, find landscape) → manually build next prompt → optionally inject synthesis (checkbox, bias warning: "last voice in the ear") → Pressure Test from hop (hard lens, examine the question) → fire → wander without pressure test (lights off, serendipity, risk of getting lost) OR Pressure Test after (lights on, flare, find the rope) → Still Open items → next prompt informed by what survived or what got lost

The wandering without pressure test is legitimate: Going in without pressure test = following a thread with no map. Risk of getting lost. Risk of finding something nobody was looking for. The serendipity is the point. Pressure test as flare = when lost, or when something has been found and needs to hold under scrutiny. Not a map — a waypoint. Survived, Destabilized, Still Open tells you what's solid under your feet.

Prompt pressure test is the hardcore version of shower thought: Shower thought builds an idea forward through sequential voices — generative, moves forward. Prompt pressure test tears the question apart before it goes anywhere — interrogative, moves inward. Both sequential in structure. Opposite in direction.

UI distinction:

Pressure Test from hop: button appears in prompt area, fires BEFORE Send. Target = the prompt itself.
Pressure Test after synthesis: button appears in synthesis panel, fires AFTER synthesis. Target = the synthesis output.
Same mode selector label, different placement, different target made explicit in the UI.
Why v1.5: Prompt pressure test is a prompt engineering change, not an architecture change. A new prompt template in run_synthesis() that targets the researcher's question rather than model responses. The synthesis model reads the prompt and asks: what assumptions does this contain, what is this question not asking, what would a rigorous examiner challenge here? Output: Assumed · Unasked · Examiner Challenges. Three sections, same rendering pipeline as Survey and Pressure Test.

The synthesis inject flow: Survey runs → researcher reads → manually builds next prompt → checkbox: "Add synthesis as context" → bias warning fires: "Synthesis was generated by [model]. Pressure test will respond to that interpretation, not the original responses. This may amplify [model]'s framing." → researcher proceeds with awareness. Optional, researcher-controlled, never automatic.

The stateless model is not the problem (June 2026)

XDA published a piece (June 26 2026) titled "I ran my local LLM for hours and watched it get dumber in real time." The diagnosis was correct: KV cache bloat from a 262K context window set to maximum on a 32GB RTX 5090, spilling into system RAM, the model's weights unchanged but the scaffold collapsing around it. The fix: new chat, reload the model, fresh start.

Marginalia uses keep_alive: 0 by design. Every model fires cold. The statelessness was chosen on epistemological grounds — the researcher assembles context deliberately, the canonical record holds state, models stay processors not memory — and turns out to be correct on engineering grounds too. No KV cache accumulation, no degradation, no 16GB of context bloat on a 16GB machine. The XDA author's fix is what Marginalia does structurally, every time, by default.

The deeper point: before frontier session memory, cloud models had the same constraint. The Marginalia process of building complete context by hand every time was never a workaround for local model limitations. It was the correct practice for all stateless models, local or cloud. The researcher is the memory. The canonical record is the memory. The model is the processor that works on what you hand it.

On the chest-puffing. The XDA piece is representative of a whole genre: the restriction is framed as a hardware problem, the solution is always more VRAM, faster RAM, a bigger GPU. A 5090 wasn't enough, so get a Strix Halo with 128GB unified memory. The conversation never asks whether the setup is wrong — only whether the hardware is big enough to hide it.

This is software brain applied to hardware. The assumption is that constraints are problems to be overpowered rather than signals to be read. Good design starts from restrictions. The constraint that a 16GB Mac Mini cannot hold a 262K context window is not a limitation to engineer around — it is the parameter that produces the right practice. You build context deliberately. You hand the model exactly what it needs. You don't accumulate garbage until the scaffold falls apart.

The Eames chair was not a failure of materials. The restriction was the instruction.

Multi-author provenance — the ethics board argument in the UI (June 2026)

Marginalia is single-user by architecture and that is correct. The canonical record is yours. The provenance fields track your thinking. But two legitimate situations bring someone else's handwritten work into the system:

Research data from a colleague — a PhD cohort member takes notes on a shared topic on her iPad. Her notes arriving via svgz or PDF are not Notes (your thinking) and not References (published work). They are community-sourced data — the same category the ethics board schema was designed for. The authors, capture_context, and your_role fields already exist in the schema for exactly this reason. Writing projects smaller than a dissertation — Marginalia supports any writing project. A conference paper, a collaborative piece, a course module. These may have co-authors, advisors, or participants whose contributions need to be attributed correctly. The platform is not dissertation-only even if the current researcher's use case is.

The build implication for v1.7 — the handwritten file import path needs a "whose work is this?" branch before saving. Your own capture: flows to Notes as now. Someone else's: prompts for author name, capture context, and your role, then lands with provenance flags intact. The authors field already exists. The accessibility angle — a colleague who takes notes digitally on an iPad can contribute to a shared research record without needing to install or understand Marginalia. That is the correct distribution of labour for a tool that asks something of its user but nothing of its participants. For a researcher working with communities whose members may not have technical fluency or institutional access, this matters.

OS update checklist addendum — cache check (June 29 2026)

After the macOS update that broke the launchd plist (see below), worth adding a standing habit: after any OS update, check disk caches before assuming all is well. Specifically du -sh ~/Library/Caches/pip and ~/.cache/pip if multiple Python installs exist. In this incident it stayed small (74MB) because the production path was fixed before the crash loop could compound, but the loop itself — pip install --upgrade firing every ~10 seconds via a broken plist's ThrottleInterval — was exactly the kind of thing that could have produced real bloat if left unnoticed for a day instead of an hour.

Not a recurring maintenance ritual. A quick check folded into the existing post-update routine: confirm launchd jobs survived, confirm ports are correct, glance at cache sizes while you're already in the terminal looking at logs.

The bootstrap.sh / production plist incident (June 29 2026)

A macOS update on Solaris caused the launchd job for Marginalia to drop out of the launchd database (clean plist, no quarantine flag, Input/output error on both launchctl load and bootstrap until a bootout cleared a ghost registration). Once reloaded, the job ran but crash-looped — launchctl list showed exit code 1, repeating every ~10 seconds via ThrottleInterval.

Root cause, once traced through /tmp/marginalia.error.log: the plist's ProgramArguments pointed at bootstrap.sh, the public repo's generic Linux/cross-platform onboarding script, not a dedicated production startup path. bootstrap.sh uses ss for port-checking, which doesn't exist on macOS, so the in-use check silently failed and the script defaulted to port 5000 — which macOS reserves for AirPlay Receiver since Monterey. The "Address already in use" error was real and specific; the generic Linux tooling just couldn't detect it correctly.

The actual fix: stop running production through any bootstrap script at all. app.py already reads MARGINALIA_PORT from the environment directly (os.environ.get("MARGINALIA_PORT", 5000)) — the plist's ProgramArguments now calls the venv's Python interpreter and app.py directly, bypassing the shell wrapper entirely. No port-hunting logic, no ss dependency, no AirPlay collision, no pip install --upgrade running on every restart cycle.

Lesson for the architecture: bootstrap scripts are first-run human setup tools. They should never be load-bearing for unattended, machine-managed restarts. The distinction needs to be explicit in HANDOFF going forward — production launchd plists call the interpreter and entry point directly, full stop.

Build follow-up: split bootstrap.sh into bootstrap-macos.sh and bootstrap-linux.sh (or a thin OS-detecting dispatcher) so the public repo's onboarding experience is correct per-platform, since ss vs lsof is a real difference a stranger cloning the repo on a Mac would hit identically.

OS updates don't break systems. Designer oversight does. (June 29 2026)

The macOS update that triggered the bootstrap.sh incident did almost nothing wrong. It dropped one launchd registration — a known, mundane occurrence with a known fix. Everything else that broke was already latent: a production plist pointed at a script that was never meant to be production-critical, written assuming Linux tooling, silently failing its own safety check instead of erroring loudly. The update didn't create the flaw. It was just the event that finally exercised a code path that had been wrong since it was written.

The system worked for months by accident — the production path happened not to touch the broken logic until a routine event made it touch it. Same shape as the XDA entry, read from the other direction. There: don't blame the hardware for a design problem. Here: don't blame the OS update for a design problem. Both are instances of looking at the wrong layer for the cause. The constraint (ss not existing on macOS) was never unusual or new. It was simply never checked.

Consensus and concept-adjacent discovery (June 30 2026)

A Consensus.app case study (PhD student, OISE/University of Toronto, studying parasocial relationships and political polarization) landed by email and is worth reading not as competition but as independent validation of a shared instinct from the other direction. Her own words on why she trusts the tool: it shows exactly where claims come from and lets her decide what the literature means. That is the same commitment academic_sources.py makes structural — source stamped, never confabulated, failover transparent — arrived at independently by a researcher describing what she actually wants from a tool, not by reading Marginalia's design docs.

Marginalia should not design against Consensus, and should not try to become it. Consensus is cloud SaaS, sold to 10,000+ universities, searching a proprietary indexed corpus server-side. That is a different product built on a different trust model — the difference is the point, not a gap to close. Marginalia's local-first, source-transparent architecture is the actual differentiator and should stay the actual differentiator.

What's worth taking seriously is the feature, examined plainly rather than competitively, because it clarifies a real distinction in Marginalia's own roadmap that wasn't fully separated before.

Concept-based discovery vs. citation-graph confirmation — two different things, not one.

The network map panel (built v1.x, lives in the Intelligence tab) does surname matching: after a synthesis runs, it scans the model's output text for author surnames that match references already in canonical, and surfaces "these were apparently drawn on." This is retrospective and closed-set — it only ever points at things already in the library. It answers "what did I already have that got used."

Consensus's concept-linking does something structurally different: embedding-space search across a large corpus, surfacing papers that share no vocabulary with the query but are conceptually adjacent — the example given was political science's "trust" language surfacing for a psychology query about "parasocial relationships." This is prospective and open-set. It answers "what exists that I don't have and didn't know to look for."

These were at risk of being conflated as "we already have something network-ish." They are not the same feature and a future build should not pretend they are. The network map confirms; concept search would discover.

What a local-only version could look like, if built: not a cloud index, not a proprietary corpus — embeddings generated locally over the abstracts already sitting in canonical/references/ (every Semantic Scholar/OpenAlex fetch already returns one), plus whatever the researcher pastes into Ingest. A local embedding model run against that corpus, with a plain-language query box, returning conceptually nearby canonical entries — and, more ambitiously, conceptually nearby entries pulled live from Semantic Scholar/OpenAlex by sending the query terms outward rather than requiring exact keyword match. The second half is the real Consensus-equivalent capability and the harder build; the first half (semantic search over what's already in canonical) is a smaller, immediately buildable step that delivers real value on its own.

Not urgent. Worth a place on the roadmap as a distinct line item — "concept-adjacent discovery" — kept clearly separate from the network map in any future planning conversation, so the two don't get merged into one underspecified feature that does neither job well.

A third mechanism, also distinct: citation-scraping from synthesis output. Long-planned, not yet built — the system reads a model's synthesis response, finds any sources or authors named in the prose, and offers one-click "Add to library" via DOI lookup or plaintext parse. This can incidentally surface far-flung, cross-disciplinary connections, but only when the underlying model happens to draw on something adjacent and names it out loud. It's harvesting citations from generated text after the fact — passive and dependent on what a model chose to mention — not actively searching a corpus the way concept-search would. Three distinct mechanisms now named clearly: network map (confirms canonical refs used in a synthesis), citation-scraping (captures new refs a model happened to mention), concept-search (deliberately discovers conceptually adjacent material regardless of what any model says). Worth keeping these three separate in any future roadmap conversation rather than letting "we get some of this already" flatten them into one underspecified feature.

Where Prompt Pressure Test fits, and where it doesn't. Pressure Test (ASSUMED / UNASKED / EXAMINER CHALLENGES) interrogates the researcher's question itself before anything fires — the closest thing Marginalia has to actively probing for blind spots rather than passively waiting for one to surface. Between network map, citation-scraping, and Pressure Test, "have I missed an angle in my own reasoning" is reasonably well covered.

The real limit: Pressure Test reasons against whatever the chosen model already knows from training. It can surface "you haven't considered X angle" only if X is something the model has internalized. It cannot say "there's a 2026 paper from a discipline you've never searched that's directly relevant" — that requires checking an actual current corpus, which nothing in Marginalia does yet. Pressure Test interrogates thinking against a model's internal knowledge; concept-search would interrogate thinking against the literature itself. Close, genuinely useful overlap — but not the same coverage, and concept-search still earns its keep as a distinct future feature rather than being subsumed by what Pressure Test already does.

Resolution path — three options, staged.

Option 1 (next build, cheapest, real value now): when Pressure Test flags an UNASKED angle, fire a Semantic Scholar/OpenAlex search using the flagged angle as the query, surface whatever returns as a quick addendum. Reuses academic_sources.py entirely — no new infrastructure, no embeddings, no model changes. Still keyword-driven, so it inherits the vocabulary-mismatch problem (won't catch a cross-discipline "trust" vs "parasocial" case) — but closes the most painful version of the gap immediately: a flagged blind spot gets checked against real literature on the spot, rather than left as an open question with no path to verify it.

Option 2 (mid-term): local embeddings over canonical/references/ only — small local model via Ollama (nomic-embed-text class), embeddings stored alongside reference files, Pressure Test's UNASKED angles checked against the existing library by concept rather than keyword. Real concept-search, but closed-set — only ever surfaces what's already in canonical, can say "you have something related" but not "something exists you don't have."

Option 3 (the real target, build last): full concept-search against Semantic Scholar/OpenAlex — embed the query, embed candidate papers pulled by a broad keyword search, rank by similarity rather than exact match. This is the actual Consensus-equivalent capability and the only option that closes the gap completely. Meaningfully expands academic_sources.py's responsibilities — embedding step on both sides, similarity ranking layer — so it should be built once 1 and 2 have proven out the simpler pieces, not attempted first.

Sequencing: 1 now (small, immediate, low-risk), 2 once canonical has enough reference volume to make local concept-search worth the build, 3 once 1 and 2 have validated the workflow and the appetite for it. Each stage stands alone and delivers real value — none of them is wasted work if the project stops at that stage.

A concrete confabulation, caught (June 30 2026)

A real case worth keeping on record rather than letting the abstract risk stay theoretical. The reference "In Praise of Idleness" (Russell, 1935) was created June 10 — before academic_sources.py existed (shipped v1.6.0, June 30). No TLDR field, no abstract, source_type: book, no DOI. Annotation ran with nothing to ground against but title and author.

Both DeepSeek and Cohere, independently, invented the same specific false detail: that Russell spent time in Singapore and drew on that experience in the essay. He didn't. Nothing in his actual biography or in the essay supports this. Two different models converged on the same fabricated specific.

This matters because of what it reveals about Survey mode's core assumption. The design logic is: run multiple models, compare outputs, disagreement reveals unreliable claims. Here, agreement was the trap — both models drew on the same kind of vague training-data association (Russell did write and travel internationally; "intellectual traveled, said things about society" is a shape confident-sounding confabulation likes to fill) and produced a false consensus that read as corroboration rather than as two independent failures landing on the same wrong answer.

The fix isn't a model problem, it's a grounding problem. This reference predates the abstract-sourcing pipeline entirely. A model reasoning from a real Semantic Scholar/OpenAlex abstract is structurally less likely to invent specifics, because it has something real to anchor to instead of free-associating from title and author alone. The TLDR field isn't just a convenience — it's load-bearing for annotation accuracy, not just for speed.

Practical implication: any reference created before v1.6.0 (June 30 2026) has no TLDR grounding and is at the same risk this one demonstrated. Worth a pass — not urgent, but real — checking which canonical references predate the academic_sources pipeline and have no TLDR, since those are the ones where annotation output should be treated with more scrutiny, not less, regardless of how confident or how well multiple models agree.

RAG-grounded Generate for non-indexed sources, and document storage as a future structural question (June 30 2026)

Surfaced directly from the Pieper Enrich test: when Semantic Scholar/OpenAlex genuinely have nothing (common for older books, essays, pre-digital-era philosophy — exactly Pieper and Russell's category), Generate currently has two options: fail honestly (Enrich's current behavior) or reason from title alone (Generate's current behavior, the actual confabulation risk this whole thread has been chasing). There's a real middle path being left on the table.

RAG-seeding Generate from open web content, distinct from Enrich's structured-abstract path. Rather than Generate reasoning about a bare title, pull whatever real text is findable — reviews, excerpts, publisher summaries, the kind of material a plain web search surfaces (confirmed directly: searching Pieper's title turned up substantial real excerpt text, scholarly reviews, and verifiable biographical/publication facts, even though no structured Semantic Scholar abstract exists for the primary work). Feed that real text to Generate as grounding context, the same role a Semantic Scholar abstract plays today. The model is now summarizing/reasoning from something real in front of it, not free-associating from a title — the same epistemic operation as Enrich's grounding, just sourced more loosely.

The honesty requirement is the part that has to be built carefully. This is not the same as a structured academic abstract and must never be stamped as one. The provenance chain gets a new, explicitly different stamp — something like "TLDR (web-sourced excerpts, tidied by [model], [date])" — visibly distinct from "TLDR (Semantic Scholar)". The researcher needs to see at a glance which kind of grounding any given TLDR has: structured-abstract (highest confidence), web-RAG'd-and-tidied (real text, but secondary/excerpted, model-synthesized), or none (blank, human-written only). Three tiers, three visible stamps, never collapsed into one undifferentiated "AI Annotation" label the way the field currently reads.

Document storage — the bigger structural question, not for now. The sharper version of "give Generate something real to read" is: what if the actual source document lived in canonical, attached to its Reference card? A PDF, a scanned excerpt, anything — then RAG-for-TLDR becomes trivially honest, since the model is summarizing a document genuinely sitting in canonical, not reaching out to the open web for secondary description of a work it can't see directly. This also closes an independent gap: right now Marginalia has no first-class concept of "the source document itself," only the markdown reference card describing it. The OCR pipeline already does ingestion of scanned pages into Notes — the architecture for "Marginalia holds a document" partially exists, just not wired to References as an attachment.

For now, the honest minimum is what already exists: physical_holding / holding_location fields let the researcher note where their own copy lives (shelf, Downloads, Zotero) without Marginalia storing the file itself. That's sufficient today. Real document storage as a Reference attachment is a future build — touches storage architecture, the OCR pipeline, and the TLDR provenance chain all at once, worth its own design pass rather than bolting onto the next quick patch.

Sequencing relative to the existing roadmap: RAG-seeded Generate for non-indexed sources is a natural extension of v1.7's canonical-informed search signal work (same area: pulling external grounding material, leads-only/context-only output, researcher decides). Document-as-attachment is bigger, later — v1.9 territory or beyond, alongside Tier 3 conceptual resonance, since both touch what canonical is allowed to hold and how deeply Marginalia reaches into a source rather than just describing it.

Source selection must be a human gate, not automatic. Confirmed in conversation, worth treating as a hard requirement for this feature rather than a nice-to-have: the search step should surface candidate sources (title, URL, snippet) and let the researcher choose which to fetch and summarize from, rather than the system automatically picking "the first result" or "the top N" and feeding them to the model silently. This matters more here than it does for Crossref leads, because RAG-seeded output writes directly into the TLDR field — the layer everything downstream (Pressure Test, synthesis, eventual concept-search) treats as trustworthy grounding. An automatically-selected low-quality source (a bad blog post, a secondhand misreading) stamped into TLDR is arguably worse than today's honest "nothing found" failure, because it would look authoritative without earning it. Same gate pattern as everywhere else in academic_sources.py: machine surfaces candidates, human judges which are worth trusting, only then does anything get written into canonical.

Enrich's false-success bug, and what it revealed (June 30 2026)

A second confirmed confabulation today, more serious than the first: Enrich returned HTTP 200 for the Ken Bain reference but wrote nothing real to the file -- no source stamp, no abstract, the Annotation section went straight from placeholder to the multi-voice synthesis output. Generate then ran completely ungrounded and DeepSeek invented a specific, false sample size ("12 instructors") with the same confident, plausible-sounding fabrication shape as the Russell/Singapore case two days earlier. Same failure mode, second instance, different model, different book.

Root cause, traced to source: both fetch_from_semantic_scholar's search path and fetch_from_openalex's search path returned the first/top search result and normalized it without checking whether that result actually had a usable abstract. The DOI-lookup paths in both functions already had this check; only the title-search paths were missing it. A title search with no relevance threshold can match a tangential or wrong record -- a citing paper, a different edition, an unrelated work that happens to share keywords -- and the old code would return that record's empty or wrong abstract as if Enrich had succeeded.

Fixed: both search paths now require abstract (Semantic Scholar) or a successfully-reconstructed abstract from abstract_inverted_index (OpenAlex) before returning a result. Empty-abstract matches now correctly fall through to "not found," matching the honest-failure behavior Pieper got two days ago.

A second, more subtle problem named alongside the fix: even with the empty-abstract guard in place, a title search can still match the wrong real paper -- something with a genuine abstract, just not the one for this specific reference. A wrong-but-real abstract is arguably worse than no abstract, because it looks trustworthy. Added a blunt word-overlap sanity check: if the matched record's title shares no words longer than 3 characters with the reference's own title, Enrich now surfaces a visible mismatch warning in the UI rather than writing silently. This is not a rigorous match-confidence system -- it will miss subtle wrong-matches and could in principle false-positive on legitimately different-but-correct title phrasing -- but it catches the clearest cases and, more importantly, it surfaces the matched title to the researcher every time now, so a human is always able to sanity-check what actually got matched before trusting it. A more rigorous match-confidence approach (DOI-first always, fuzzy title similarity scoring, author-overlap confirmation) is a future task, not today's fix.

The sharper diagnostic, stated directly in conversation and worth keeping verbatim: "The mechanical elements are just trying to be bombastic for no other reason than trying to please." Survey mode's CONSENSUS/DIVERGENCE/UNIQUE CONTRIBUTIONS template structurally demands specificity -- methodology details, sample sizes, points of disagreement between models -- regardless of whether either model has real grounding for any of it. A looser, ungrounded query to Gemini on the same book produced general, accurate, unembellished content with no fabricated specifics, because nothing in that simpler prompt shape pressured it toward inventing precision it didn't have. This is worth treating as a structural finding about Survey mode itself, not just a grounding-data problem: the template's demand for comparative specificity may be applying confabulation pressure independent of which model runs it or how well-grounded the input is.

Where this leaves Generate's trustworthiness, stated plainly: as of today, Generate's output should not be trusted for any factual specifics (sample sizes, dates, biographical or methodological detail) regardless of verification status, until grounding (Enrich, RAG-seeding, or real document attachment) is confirmed actually present and confirmed actually correct for that specific reference. Verification status alone does not vouch for annotation accuracy -- it only vouches that the reference itself is real. These are different claims and the UI does not yet distinguish them clearly. Worth folding into the TLDR provenance tiers work already queued (v1.6.7) -- a reference being "Verified" and a TLDR being "grounded" need to read as two separate, independently-true-or-false facts, not one conflated status.

The deploy script's restart step never caught up to launchd (June 30 2026)

A second process-management incident today, same root cause shape as the bootstrap.sh incident two days earlier: two different ownership models for the same process, never reconciled.

The deploy one-liner's restart step (pkill -9 -f "python.*app.py" followed by manual nohup .venv/bin/python app.py &) predates the launchd fix from two days ago. It made sense when nothing else was managing the process -- you killed it, you restarted it, simple. Once launchd took over as the actual process owner (KeepAlive: true, RunAtLoad: true), that same pattern started fighting against it instead: the deploy's pkill -9 kills launchd's child process, which launchd interprets as a crash and may attempt its own restart per KeepAlive, while the deploy script's own nohup is racing to start a separate, unmanaged instance at the same time. Depending on timing, one wins, the other silently loses, or both die. Today's pattern: several deploys in a row appeared to succeed (files copied, commits made, output looked clean) while the actual running process quietly stayed on an older version or stopped entirely, surfacing as launchctl list showing exit code -9 (killed) and ps aux showing nothing running at all.

Worth naming the pattern explicitly since it's the second time in three days: a process or script written under one set of ownership assumptions kept being reused unmodified after those assumptions changed, and nothing forced a review of whether it was still correct. bootstrap.sh assumed manual human startup; it kept running unattended under launchd. The deploy script's pkill/nohup pair assumed it was the only thing managing the process; it kept running after launchd took ownership. Same shape, same fix in spirit: once a process is owned by a supervisor, every interaction with that process needs to go through the supervisor, not around it.

The fix: deploy's restart step now calls sudo launchctl kickstart -k gui/$(id -u rajboora)/com.marginalia.server instead of pkill+nohup. This asks launchd to restart its own managed job directly -- no race, no competing process, no silent failure mode where the deploy script and launchd disagree about what should be running. Costs one sudo password prompt per deploy, which is a small, honest price for correctness over the alternative of silently serving stale code while believing the deploy succeeded.

Diagnostic value of the symptom, worth remembering: the live server kept answering curl requests with an old version string even when ps aux showed nothing running -- almost certainly a stale cache (browser, Tailscale, or otherwise) rather than a phantom server, which is worth checking for specifically next time something looks "running but wrong" rather than assuming the process itself is lying.

Single-thread depth vs. council breadth -- and why the corpus changes everything (June 30 2026)

A control experiment today: the same Varela/enaction prompt run through Marginalia's Survey mode (seven models, structured CONSENSUS/DIVERGENCE output) and through a single frontier model in a sibling thread. The sibling thread produced the sharper, more dissertation-relevant response -- specifically the autopoiesis-as-threat-response connection ("if the student's autopoietic organization is running a threat response, the perturbations the teacher offers get filtered out as noise before they can become learning") which none of the seven models surfaced despite having the same prompt.

Why this happens, stated plainly: a frontier model with a large context window can hold the full thread, notice which part of the researcher's thinking is sharpest, and extend it in the direction that matters. The council of models runs seven parallel, stateless, single-serving passes. Each model sees the prompt cold, produces its best survey-shaped output, and exits. No model in the council can say "your second observation is the sharper and more original move" and then build on it -- because no model in the council has read the first observation in relation to the second. They've each read only the final prompt block.

"Single serving friends" is the precise term for what the council currently is. Useful for breadth, for catching absent voices, for surfaces the researcher hasn't considered. Not useful for depth, for extending the sharpest idea, for the kind of push-back that produces dissertation-ready language. These are different jobs and knowing which to reach for at which moment is part of the research practice the tool is trying to support.

The corpus changes this. Right now the council has no shared memory of the research project. Once canonical reaches sufficient density -- references with grounded abstracts, sessions with concept tags, notes with genuine annotation -- synthesis and intelligence passes run against that corpus rather than against cold prompt text alone. A model that has read the researcher's annotated Varela note, their Battiste synthesis, their autopoiesis concept card, and their dissertation argument connection before it sees the new prompt is no longer a single-serving friend. It's operating with something closer to the context a long-running conversation partner would have. That's the version of the council worth building toward, and it's exactly what the v1.7 canonical-informed search signal, the session auto-titling, and the concept tags are all pointing at from different directions.

Until then: frontier model for depth and extension, council for breadth and gap-checking. Both have real jobs. Neither replaces the other.

Kintsugi mode -- from crack identification to crack pursuit (June 30 2026)

Pressure Test names the cracks. Kintsugi mode fills them with gold -- not answers, but prompts worth pursuing.

The problem it solves: Pressure Test surfaces ASSUMED, UNASKED, EXAMINER CHALLENGES and then leaves them sitting there. The researcher sees the cracks, maybe files them, moves on. Nothing follows a crack into the depth it deserves. The sibling thread follows cracks because it has context to know which one is load-bearing -- it noticed "your second observation is the sharper move" and went through that crack specifically rather than listing all of them equally. Pressure Test lists. A context-holding interlocutor prioritizes and pursues. Kintsugi mode is the bridge.

Three-step shape:

Name -- Pressure Test already does this. ASSUMED / UNASKED / EXAMINER CHALLENGES. The cracks are identified.

Outline -- a second pass that takes the named cracks and selects the most load-bearing one. Not all cracks are equal. "You haven't defined what counts as intellectual risk-taking" is a bigger crack than "your sample size isn't specified yet." The Kintsugi pass picks the one worth filling first -- the crack where the dissertation actually lives.

Fill with prompts -- not answers, not synthesis. A set of offered questions the researcher can take into a deep session. "Leonard Cohen lives" is the register: questions that have some pull to them, not academic hedging or bullet points. "What would a student who has never felt safe in an institution say about your Step Zero?" "Where does autopoiesis end and performance begin?" "Who gets to decide when the perturbation was learning and when it was harm?" The researcher picks the prompt that has the most charge for them right now, takes it to a frontier thread or fires it through the council with a reference attached, and goes deep.

Why the metaphor is load-bearing: kintsugi doesn't hide the cracks or patch them over with synthesis. The gold fill is the researcher's attention directed deliberately into the break. The break is where the dissertation lives. A mode named after this isn't decorative -- it names the epistemological claim that the most generative place to work is exactly where the argument is most fragile.

The enactive moment: Kintsugi mode surfaces the options. The researcher choosing which prompt to follow is the enactive moment -- not receiving an answer but orienting toward the next question. That's the gut feeling of where to go, applied to the research process itself.

Sequencing: natural extension of Pressure Test, same synthesis infrastructure, different output shape. The "fill with prompts" pass could run through whichever model handles the EXAMINER CHALLENGES well -- Gemma has been consistently good at naming the sharpest challenge, worth routing Kintsugi's prioritization pass through it specifically. Good v1.7-1.8 candidate once the corpus starts providing grounding material for the offered prompts to draw from.

Claude Sonnet 4.6 read the full note correctly, including the lower-half connections that Gemma missed, and independently identified the Simmons-Odell-Varela cluster without being prompted toward it. This is relevant for two reasons: first, it confirms the content was recoverable from the image with a capable enough model; second, it identifies a real gap in the local OCR pipeline -- Gemma 4 is the current OCR workhorse for handwritten ingest, and it has a legibility threshold below which connections get lost. Fast, compressed handwriting from a thinking-in-motion moment is exactly the input that matters most and exactly the input Gemma struggles with most.

The Stylus Write integration (v1.7 ingest, scoped in seeds above) becomes more compelling in this light: digital ink preserves stroke quality and line grouping that a camera-to-JPEG flattens, giving the OCR model a structurally better signal regardless of handwriting speed. For a researcher who writes fast and lets legibility suffer for pace, digital ink is a meaningfully better input than photographed notebook paper.

Interim option worth considering: route handwritten image OCR through a frontier model (Gemini, Sonnet) rather than Gemma when the image quality is poor or the content is high-stakes. Slower and costs tokens, but catches what Gemma misses. Could be a toggle in the ingest UI -- "careful OCR" vs "fast OCR" -- rather than a permanent switch.

OCR signal confidence selector -- local first, frontier on request (June 30 2026)

The Gemma/Sonnet comparison on the napkin note suggests a practical ingest improvement: route OCR through local models by default, offer frontier model OCR when the researcher flags the input as difficult. Not a quality dial -- a signal-confidence dial. The researcher self-assesses the handwriting before OCR fires, and the system routes accordingly.

Three states, not a continuous slider:

Clean -- typed text, digital ink, printed documents. No OCR needed for text-layer PDFs. Gemma handles image OCR trivially. Fast, local, free.

Legible -- clear handwriting, deliberate notes. Gemma handles this well. Default for most handwritten ingest. Try local first; offer frontier retry if the result looks thin or missing connections.

Difficult -- fast notes, compressed thinking, the "worst doctor" end of the spectrum. Gemma loses the intellectually interesting parts -- exactly the compressed, accelerated writing where the most generative thinking lives. Route to Gemini or Sonnet with a token cost warning. Worth it for high-stakes notes; the researcher decides.

The "worst doctor" pole is the asymptotic case -- probably nothing recovers it reliably, human transcription is the honest answer at that end. The selector doesn't promise recovery, it just allocates the right tool for the signal quality.

Implementation: a small three-state toggle in the ingest UI before OCR fires -- Clean / Legible / Difficult -- not a full slider. Default is Legible (Gemma). Difficult triggers frontier model with explicit token cost shown before confirming. Clean skips OCR entirely for text-layer inputs. The researcher makes one judgment call about their own handwriting; the system handles the routing.

This also applies to the Stylus Write (.svgz) integration -- digital ink is structurally Clean regardless of how fast the writing was, since stroke grouping is preserved. One more argument for the Write pipeline over camera-to-JPEG for researchers who write fast.

The commons test -- finishing vs. imagining (July 2 2026)

A real, reusable filter, worth keeping distinct from scope-creep and applying deliberately rather than by instinct alone.

The distinction: once something is public, there's a real obligation not to leave it with dangling bugs or gaps that make it broken for people who are already using it. That's different in kind from imagining hypothetical future users and building speculatively for needs nobody has actually expressed. The first is finishing what you shipped. The second is scope inflation wearing a generous face.

The test: does this addition make the tool complete for people who already showed up, or does it make it speculatively more for people who haven't shown up and might never? Accessibility tags are the first kind -- real people using the tool right now are affected, skipping them means the tool is quietly broken for a real fraction of real users, regardless of whether any specific person has filed a complaint. A hypothetical multi-format citation exporter for a hypothetical LaTeX user who hasn't asked is the second kind -- it's imagining a need into existence rather than responding to one.

A third category, worth naming separately: contributing to a commons even when not asked, when the cost is small and the addition helps more people, or helps someone specific you actually know who's expressed real interest. This is neither "finishing what's broken" nor "imagining hypothetical demand" -- it's a genuine act of generosity, offered because it's cheap and the tool is already public, not because anyone requested it. The accessibility work and the RSIF/OKF public release both belong here: nobody demanded either, but the cost was proportionate to real people who exist (disabled users of any public tool; researchers on other platforms who might genuinely benefit from a portable format), not to an imagined market.

Applied to the APA/citation-export decision just made: APA 7 is real, confirmed, personally needed -- build it properly, finish it, no dangling gaps once it ships. Multi-format export for people who haven't asked is imagined-need territory -- correctly deferred, not because it's a bad idea but because it hasn't earned its place yet. If someone specific -- a colleague, a lab-mate, anyone real -- actually says "I wish this exported to BibTeX," that request moves it from imagined into the third category: small, real, worth doing generously even though it wasn't originally scoped.

Worth applying this three-way test (finish what's broken / don't imagine demand / do give generously when it's cheap and real) to every future "should I add this" question, not just citation formats.

v1.6.11 — build session observations (July 23 2026)

The prompt stacking bug and what it revealed

The +++ context separator has always been a frontend-only convention — getActivePrompt() in app.js extracts only the final block after the last +++ and that's what gets sent to all models. The intent was to send context layers to the server and have the server handle them, but in practice prompt: in the fetch body was calling getActivePrompt() (final block only) while full_prompt: (the complete raw value including all context layers) was only ever written to the canonical session file — never sent to the models. Fix: prompt: now sends the full raw value. getActivePrompt() is still correct for the token count display and nothing else.

The symptom that surfaced the bug: Llama (last in MODEL_ORDER, last to fire in the sequential local queue) was explicitly saying "no chain of thought provided" while every other model responded to the accumulated context. This looked like a Llama-specific issue but wasn't — every model was getting only the final block, and Llama's response happened to be the one that made the absence explicit rather than working around it.

The .response-body selector that never existed

highlightAuthorsInResponses() was querying .response-body — a class that doesn't exist anywhere in the markup. makeCard() has always created .response-text. The highlight function has likely never fired on a single response card. Fixed to .response-text.

Modal closes on click-outside — design principle

The edit modal closing on backdrop click was onclick="if(event.target===this)closeEditModal()" on the overlay div in index.html. Removed. The principle: modals that contain text fields and multi-step workflows (enrich search → candidate selection → abstract write) must require explicit cancel gesture. Unexpected dismissal loses work silently. A cancel button is visible; the backdrop is not a cancel button.

silentSave and the two-tab problem

silentSave() fires every 30 minutes via setInterval. With two tabs open, both intervals run independently — two git commits per 30 minutes, both with "Auto-save" messages. Added document.hidden guard: if the tab is not the active/visible one, silentSave skips. The active tab commits; the background tab stays quiet.

Refs chunk in synthesis panel

New section below synthesis output: surfaces author surnames that appeared across model responses and synthesis, with frequency count and library status (green = verified, yellow = located/surfaced, rose = not in library). Names already present in the researcher's prompt are discounted — they're known context, not discoveries. Rose names are clickable and route to the References tab with the name pre-filled in search. This is the beginning of a tighter loop between what models surface and what the researcher adds to canonical.

Dedup DOI hierarchy, DOI 404 bare record, already-in-library badge, import SSE heartbeat (October 21 2026, v1.8.16)

Dedup detection now uses a DOI-primary hierarchy: exact DOI match (excluding placeholders like "none", "n/a", "-") takes priority, then falls back to author+year+title token overlap (≥3 tokens).

Enrich redesign — from auto-select to candidate picker (June 30 2026, stabilized through v1.8.x)

The original Enrich was a single-shot operation: search the index, take the top hit, write it into canonical. The Ken Bain false-success bug (documented in Seeds, June 30 2026) killed that design. The replacement is a two-step flow with a human gate:

Step 1: `/api/references/<ref>/enrich/search` — searches both indices, returns CANDIDATES. Writes nothing.

Step 2: `/api/references/<ref>/enrich/confirm` — researcher picks a candidate, confirms, and only then does the write happen.

A third path: `/api/references/<ref>/enrich/doi-refetch` — for refs with known DOIs but stale metadata. No candidate selection needed — the human gate was the DOI itself.

Design laid down in v1.6.8, stabilization through v1.8.x.

Rich document import — rsif-x and hyperlinked sources (October 28 2026, noted)

Marginalia currently ingests flat formats (BibTeX, RIS, CSV, DOI batch, plaintext). ## 2.0 Definition (October 2026)

2.0 is the ability to start a project, assign the framework (onion), link notes/sessions/refs, create a network map, and use the refs/notes/session ideas to assemble a short presentation — the full research lifecycle in one tool.

### The Marginalia Graph

The central artifact of 2.0 and potentially the PhD contribution itself. Not a citation network, not a concept map, not a social graph — it's the epistemic topology of one researcher's path through a body of literature and thinking, with dead ends, tensions, and leaps intact.

**Key properties:**
- **Living, not generated.** The graph grows over weeks/months of use. Every session, annotation, link decision adds structure. It's a research journal rendered as topology, not a report.
- **Writable first, pretty second.** The v1 map doesn't need to be beautiful. It needs to make it easy for every interaction with the tool to potentially add structure to the graph.
- **Human-first, AI-assisted.** The researcher creates the structure. AI proposes links, but nothing is written without human confirmation. The tool works without AI; AI makes it faster.
- **The onion gives it a second geometry.** The citation/connection graph is one axis. The onion positions give a radial/methodological axis. Each layer is a concentric ring; the project's position on each ring defines a methodological path. The gap between layers — "closer and closer, but never touch" — is where the interesting methodological arguments live. A project positioned as interpretivist but using statistical analysis has a visible stretch across rings. That's not an error — it's something the researcher should see and defend.
- **Exportable as image.** The researcher needs to be able to capture the current graph state as an image to share with others (supervisor, colleague, family) for outside eyes on the argument's shape.

**The workflow it enables:**
Drop a comment or idea in → fire a session against it → use the session to write notes → notice it connects to a paper → well, it did, maybe not, but there's this other thing → see the whole thing as an image → share it with someone whose wise eyes see what you can't.

That's not a feature spec. That's the actual rhythm of research thinking, and the tool has to support it without getting in the way.

### Build order to 2.0

1. **Onion** — built (v1.8.19), deploy when Solaris is reachable
2. **Map v1** — network visualization with the link model, human-created links only. No LLM proposals yet. Get the graph and the accretion model right first.
3. **Presentation assembly** — pull from the graph into structured output (short presentation, chapter draft, talk outline)
4. **RAG upgrade / LLM link proposals** — the AI layer, slotted in once the human workflow is solid. RAG is an integration point, not a core build — plug in whatever the state of the art is; it's moving fast outside this project.

### What 2.0 is NOT

- Not dependent on AI working well. If the tool only works because the AI is good, it's fragile.
- Not a one-shot visualization. The graph is grown, not generated.
- Not bibliometrics. The structure is personal and epistemic, not citation-count-based.

### Onion as map geometry

Each onion layer is a concentric ring on the visualization. The project's selections define a radial path through the onion. Two projects with similar paths are methodologically close; divergent paths are in tension. The gap between layers — epistemology vs. actual techniques — is the tension field where methodological arguments live. The onion isn't just a filter or color-code on the map; it's a second axis of structure layered on the citation/connection graph.
